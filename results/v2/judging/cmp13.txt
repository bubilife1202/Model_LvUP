=== ANSWER A ===
Raft replicates a log of commands across a cluster. Definitions, used precisely below: a **term** is a monotonically increasing integer acting as a logical clock; each term begins with a **leader election**, in which a timed-out server increments its term and solicits votes, becoming leader if a majority votes for it; each term has at most one leader. A **log index** is an entry's 1-based position in a server's log; every entry stores a command and the term of the leader that created it. **Majority** always means strictly more than half of the full cluster membership — at least 2 of 3 — counting crashed servers in the denominator. **Committed** means the entry is permanently decided; the precise rule comes later.

## Log matching

The property: if two servers' logs each contain an entry with the same index and the same term — "same term" meaning the term numbers stored inside the two entries are equal — then (a) the entries hold the same command, and (b) the logs share the same prefix, meaning that at every index from 1 through that index, both logs hold entries identical in term and command.

Claim (a) holds because a term has one leader, a leader creates at most one entry per index, and it never deletes or overwrites its own entries — so an (index, term) pair names a unique entry. Claim (b) is maintained inductively by the consistency check in AppendEntries. Each RPC carries prevLogIndex and prevLogTerm: the index and term of the leader's entry immediately preceding the entries being shipped. The follower rejects unless its log holds an entry at prevLogIndex whose stored term equals prevLogTerm. If that one entry matches, the property applied to it means the entire prefix matches, so appending after it preserves the invariant. On rejection, the leader decrements its nextIndex for that follower and retries earlier. Once the check passes, the follower deletes any of its entries that conflict with incoming ones (same index, different term) plus everything after them, then appends the leader's entries. The leader never rewrites its own log; followers converge to it.

## Leader completeness

The property: an entry committed in term T appears in the log of the leader of every term greater than T. New leaders never need to recover committed data; entries flow only from leader to follower.

The mechanism is the election restriction: a server votes for a candidate only if the candidate's log is at least as up-to-date as its own — comparing the logs' last entries, the higher last term wins; on equal last terms, the higher last index wins. A committed entry is stored on a majority, and winning an election requires votes from a majority; two majorities of one cluster always intersect, so at least one voter holds the committed entry and refuses any candidate whose log is less up-to-date than its own. The complete safety proof is inductive and leans on log matching, but that intersection argument is its heart.

## Running example: nodes A, B, C

Term 1: A is leader. Entry (index 1, term 1, "x=1") reaches B and C and is committed. A appends (index 2, term 1, "y=2") locally and crashes before sending it.

Term 2: B times out and campaigns; C votes for it (their last entries are identical), giving B a majority (B, C). Leader B appends (index 2, term 2, "y=7") and (index 3, term 2, "z=5"); C acknowledges both. Each sits on a majority and carries B's current term, so both are committed. B then crashes; A restarts with the log [1:term 1, 2:term 1].

Term 3: C campaigns; A votes for it, since C's last term (2) beats A's (1). C leads with votes from C and A. Its last index is 3, so it sends A an AppendEntries with prevLogIndex 3, prevLogTerm 2. A rejects — nothing at index 3. C retries with prevLogIndex 2, prevLogTerm 2; A rejects again — its index-2 entry stores term 1. C retries with prevLogIndex 1, prevLogTerm 1, shipping entries 2 and 3; the check passes. A deletes its conflicting (index 2, term 1, "y=2") — never committed, so safely gone — and appends C's entries. C then appends (index 4, term 3, "w=8"), replicates it to A, and commits it.

B restarts with [1:term 1, 2:term 2, 3:term 2] and current term 2; C crashes. B campaigns in term 3; A rejects — it already gave term 3's vote to C, and B's log is behind anyway. B retries in term 4; A refuses purely on the up-to-date rule: A's last entry has term 3, B's has term 2. With one vote of three, B loses — the restriction just excluded a node lacking a committed entry. A campaigns in term 5; B grants the vote (A's log wins the comparison), and A becomes leader with 2 of 3, holding every committed entry, including (index 4, term 3).

## Committed vs. merely replicated vs. applied

Replicated means an entry exists in some server's log — nothing more; A's (index 2, term 1) entry was replicated yet rightly destroyed. Committed means permanently decided: the leader marks an entry committed once an entry of its own current term is stored on a majority, which also commits everything before it; committed entries survive into all future leaders. Applied means a server has executed the entry against its state machine — always after commit, possibly lagging it; the last-applied index never exceeds the commit index, and clients observe only applied results.

## A subtle misconception

"Replicated on a majority" is not the same as "committed" when the entry came from an earlier term. A leader counts replicas toward commitment only for entries of its current term; an old-term entry on a majority can still be overwritten after a leadership change (the Raft paper's Figure 8 scenario), and commits only indirectly, once a later current-term entry reaches a majority. In the example, had B crashed before committing, C could not have declared entries 2 and 3 committed merely by seeing them on a majority — it would first need its own term-3 entry replicated to a majority.

=== ANSWER B ===
**Raft Safety Properties: Log Matching and Leader Completeness**

**Background Definitions**

A *term* is a monotonically increasing integer that acts as a logical clock; each election attempt starts a new term. A *log index* is a 1-based position in a node's append-only log of command entries. An entry is *committed* once the leader has replicated it to a majority of nodes (meaning more than half of all nodes, e.g., at least 2 of 3). An entry is *applied* when a node passes it to its state machine for execution. A *leader* is elected by majority vote and is the sole writer during its term.

---

**Log Matching Property**

Log matching states: if two nodes have an entry with the same index and the same term, then their logs are identical in all entries up to and including that index.

"Same index and same term" means the numerical log position matches exactly and the term number recorded in the entry matches exactly. "Identical in all entries up to that index" means every prior slot holds an entry with the same command and the same term — a shared prefix with no gaps or substitutions.

This property is enforced by the `AppendEntries` consistency check. Every `AppendEntries` RPC carries a `prevLogIndex` and `prevLogTerm` — the index and term of the entry immediately preceding the new entries being sent. Before appending, a follower rejects the RPC if its own log does not contain an entry at `prevLogIndex` with term equal to `prevLogTerm`. If it does match, the follower appends the new entries and overwrites any conflicting tail. The leader then retries with a decremented `prevLogIndex` until it finds a matching point.

Why does this inductive check guarantee the full prefix? At index 1, the leader sends `prevLogIndex=0`, which is vacuously satisfied. If a follower accepts entries up to index k, it has verified the entry at k−1 matches, and by induction the entire prefix up to k is consistent with the leader's log. A follower can never accept an entry at index k unless the entire prefix back to index 1 already matches.

---

**Leader Completeness Property**

Leader completeness states: if an entry is committed in some term, every leader elected in any later term has that entry in its log at the time of election.

The election restriction that enforces this is the *up-to-date vote rule*: a candidate is granted a vote only if its log is at least as up-to-date as the voter's log. "At least as up-to-date" is defined precisely: the candidate's last log entry has a higher term than the voter's last entry, or if the terms are equal, the candidate's log is at least as long (index is greater than or equal).

Why does this prevent an outdated candidate from winning? A committed entry has been stored on a majority of nodes. Any new election also requires a majority of votes. These two majorities must overlap — they share at least one node. That overlapping node holds the committed entry and will refuse to vote for a candidate whose log does not reflect it, because the candidate's log would be "less up-to-date." Since the candidate needs a majority, and at least one member of every possible majority rejects it, the candidate cannot win.

---

**Concrete Three-Node Example**

Cluster: nodes A, B, C. A is the initial leader in term 1.

A replicates three entries at indices 1, 2, 3 (all term 1) to B and C, commits all three (majority confirmed), and all nodes apply them.

A then replicates an entry at index 4 (term 1) only to B before crashing. B has index 4 but C does not. Index 4 is not yet committed.

A new election starts (term 2). C runs for leader. B's last entry is index 4, term 1. C's last entry is index 3, term 1. B compares: same last term (1), but B's log is longer (index 4 vs. 3), so C is less up-to-date. B withholds its vote. C cannot win with only its own vote (1 of 3). B wins instead, becoming leader in term 2 with its log intact through index 4.

Now consider a variant where B wins in term 2 and replicates index 4 to A and C, committing it. Later, another crash causes a term 3 election. C is still at index 3 (term 1). A and B are at index 4 (term 1, now committed). C cannot win: it needs 2 votes, but both A and B see C's log as less up-to-date (same last term 1, shorter log). Either A or B becomes leader, preserving index 4.

If C had won before index 4 was committed, it would have appended its own entries at index 4 with term 2, conflicting with B's uncommitted entry. When B rejoined, the `AppendEntries` check at index 3 would match, and B would overwrite its index 4 with the leader's term-2 entry — the log matching correction.

---

**Committed vs. Replicated vs. Applied**

*Replicated* means a copy of the entry exists on a node's log, but the leader has not yet heard back from a majority. *Committed* means the leader has confirmed the entry is durably stored on a majority and has advanced its commit index; the entry will survive any future leader change. *Applied* means the entry has been passed to the state machine. Applied always lags commit: a follower applies entries only after learning the commit index from the leader's next message.

---

**Misconception to Avoid**

A common mistake: believing that once an entry is replicated to a majority it is safe to treat as committed. In Raft, a leader may only commit an entry from its *current* term by counting majority acknowledgments; it cannot directly commit entries from prior terms by majority count alone. An old entry becomes committed only indirectly, when a new entry from the current term is committed and the commit index advances past it. Treating replication-to-majority as equivalent to commitment can lead to incorrectly concluding that an entry is durable when a new leader could still overwrite it.
